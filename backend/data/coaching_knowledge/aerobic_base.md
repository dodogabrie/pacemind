# Aerobic Base Development

## Key Principles
- Aerobic capacity (AeT) is the single most important quality for endurance performance — you will never maximize endurance without first maximizing AeT
- World-class endurance athletes spend 80-85% of training volume in Zones 1-2
- Low-intensity training adaptations are structural (capillary density, mitochondrial mass, cardiac output) — slow to build but long-lasting
- High-intensity adaptations are functional (enzyme concentrations, transporter proteins) — fast to appear but short-lived
- Volume is the key stimulus: duration is the single biggest training stimulus from any exercise

## The Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome (ADS)
### What It Is
**What:** A condition where the athlete's aerobic metabolism is underdeveloped relative to anaerobic capacity, resulting in a very slow AeT pace
**When:** Common in athletes who overuse high-intensity training (Z3-Z4) or come from HIIT/CrossFit backgrounds
**How to detect:** Use the Ten Percent Test (see intensity_zones.md). If AeT HR is more than 10% below LT HR, you have ADS
**Numbers:**
- Athletes with ADS may have AeT pace as slow as 8:30/mile (5:16/km) despite having VO2max of 90 ml/kg/min
- After 4 months of proper base training, the same athlete improved AeT pace to 6:15/mile (3:53/km) with HR rising from 135 to 158

### Cure for ADS
**What:** High volume of low-to-moderate intensity training exclusively at or below AeT
**How:**
- Do all aerobic training at or below AeT heart rate
- Accept the embarrassingly slow pace — it reflects your actual aerobic capacity
- Faking it by running faster only slows aerobic development
- Early on, you can do almost all training in Zone 2 because the pace will be slow enough
- As AeT improves and pace gets faster, shift more volume to Zone 1 (Zone 2 becomes too taxing for daily use)
- Recheck AeT every 2 weeks with a time trial at constant HR
**Numbers:** Athletes new to this may initially do 6-7 Z2 workouts/week; as pace improves, drop to 1-2/week because the neuromuscular stress increases

## Fat Adaptation
### Why It Matters
**What:** Well-trained athletes can utilize fats at much higher intensities than previously thought — up to 2x the rate of untrained
**Numbers:**
- Fat stores: ~100,000 calories available even in lean athletes
- Glycogen stores: max ~2,000 calories
- Fat produces no lactate when metabolized
- Recent studies show fat contribution continues up to maximum intensities in well-trained athletes

### How to Become Fat Adapted
**What:** Train in a way that forces the body to rely more on fat for fuel
**How:**
- Long-duration, low-intensity workouts (primary method)
- Some training in a fasted state (morning before eating) — forces muscles to use fat
- Reduced carbohydrate / increased fat diet (supplementary for those training <12 hrs/week)
- Athletes training >12 hrs/week get ample fat adaptation stimulus from volume alone

## Volume Guidelines
### How Much Is Enough
**What:** The daily aerobic base test
**Rule:** If you can't do the same workout you did yesterday again today, and tomorrow and the day after for days on end, then you are doing too much volume or too much intensity for aerobic base building
**Caveat:** If your AeT is within 10% of LT, this applies only to Zone 1 (Zone 2 will be too taxing for daily use)

### Reference Training Volumes
- Nationally competitive mountain runner/skimo racer with full-time job: avg 12.5 hrs/week, 650 hrs/year
- Peak weeks: 3 weeks over 40 hrs, 11 weeks over 23 hrs
- Average elevation: 9,843 ft/week (3,028m), peak 20,000+ ft (6,154m)
- Average distance: 55 mi/week (90 km), peak 100+ mi (160 km)
- World-class mountain runner: 1,000-1,300 hours/year, 1.5 million vertical feet/year

### Annual Volume Targets for Beginners
- Juniors (under 15): 300 hours/year
- Juniors (16-19): 450 hours/year
- Adults (20-35): 400 hours/year
- Adults (35-50): 350 hours/year
- Adults (over 50): 300 hours/year
- Increase max 25%/year for beginners (<400 hrs), max 10%/year for advanced (>500 hrs)

## Continuous Training Methods
### Zone 1-2 Base Workouts
- Minimum 30 minutes continuous, up to several hours
- Zone 1: 50-70% of annual aerobic volume
- Zone 2: used sparingly once AeT is within 10% of LT (<15% of weekly volume)
- Can carry on conversation or breathe through nose
- Blood lactate below 2 mMol/L

### Pick-Ups (Strides)
**What:** Short speed segments inserted into Z1-2 workouts
**How:** 6-8 reps of 8-15 seconds at relaxed fast pace (never full sprint), 2 min easy between reps
**When:** Start after a few weeks of aerobic base training; 1 workout/week
**Why:** Nearly universal acceptance across endurance sports for maintaining neuromuscular qualities during base phase

### Progressive Distance Workout
**What:** Start slow, gradually increase pace through zones during a long workout
**Example:** 40 min Z1 → 30 min Z2 → 20 min Z3 → 5 min Z4 (if feeling good)
**When:** One every 10-14 days after early Base Period
**Duration:** 60 min minimum, most effective beyond 90 min
**Recovery needed:** 1-2 days of recovery workouts afterward

## Elite Mountain Runner Training Distribution (example year)
- Total: ~1,300 hours
- Zone 1-2 (aerobic capacity): 675-700 hours (54%)
- Zone 3 (comfortable but fast aerobic): 350 hours (26%)
- Zones 3-4-5 (high intensity, mostly racing): 275 hours (20%)
- Note: Heavy race schedule shifted distribution toward more intensity than typical; younger years were more heavily weighted to base

## Common Mistakes
- Training too fast during base building — ego prevents accepting slow AeT pace
- Overusing Zone 3 because it "feels like training should feel" — leads to plateau then decline
- Interpreting a performance plateau as needing more intensity when actually the aerobic base has eroded
- Copying elite athletes' training without their years of capacity building
- Trying to build capacity and utilize it at the same time

## Decision Rules for the Agent
- IF AeT HR is >10% below LT HR THEN athlete has ADS — prescribe almost all training in Z1-Z2, no Z3-Z4
- IF AeT HR is within 10% of LT HR THEN athlete can add Z3 workouts (up to 10% of weekly volume)
- IF athlete can do same workout daily for days on end THEN volume/intensity is appropriate for base building
- IF athlete reports legs giving out before breathing in workouts THEN prescribe muscular endurance work
- IF athlete is new to structured training THEN start conservatively, build no more than 10%/week
